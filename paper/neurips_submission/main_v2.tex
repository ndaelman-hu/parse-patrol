\begin{abstract}
Agentic systems are entering the molecular sciences as co-scientists that plan, call
domain tools, and reason over the results of simulations. Every such system rests on
a first, unglamorous step: turning a computation's raw output files into structured
data---\textbf{parsing}. This grounding step spans a wide range of computational
complexity, yet is assumed to ``just work,'' because a coding agent can emit code in
a Turing-complete language and is therefore taken to solve processing tasks of
arbitrary difficulty. That is a claim about expressive \emph{competence}, not
realized \emph{performance}---Chomsky's own distinction. We make the assumption
explicit and measure it: we place each extraction on the language-complexity
hierarchy (Chomsky, refined by Joshi's mildly-context-sensitive band) and separate a
second factor---whether a tested grammar is \emph{supplied}. Using real
computational-chemistry output as the test case and an MCP parser harness whose
documentation we vary, we record the agent's workload, chain-of-thought, and a
failure-mode taxonomy \emph{indexed by grammatical class}. \pending{headline numbers
from the full agent sweep.} Preliminary component measurements already show the
gap---recovery falls sharply with grammatical class, decided less by grammatical
simplicity than by whether a grammar was supplied. We then show the harness is not
just diagnostic but a \emph{remedy}: supplying tested grammars and documentation
\emph{measurably mitigates the drop-off} \pending{by X}, recovering exactly the
\emph{interface} (offloadable) failures while the residual \emph{reasoning} failures
at the context-sensitive level persist. Its role is thus not to grant new expressive
power (the agent could synthesize the parser itself) but to reduce the \emph{cost} of
realizing it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\theme{the assumption}When a coding agent is dropped into a scientific workflow, we
tend to assume it can handle whatever data processing the workflow throws at it. The
reasoning is seductive: the agent writes code, code is a Turing-complete medium, and a
Turing-complete medium can express any computable transformation, so any task that is
computable at all should be within reach. The assumption is rarely stated outright,
yet it is what licenses deploying agents as general-purpose glue between scientific
tools and the data they emit.

\theme{competence vs.\ performance}It conflates two things, and the distinction
between them is old. Chomsky drew it as \emph{competence} versus \emph{performance}
\citep{chomsky1965}: what a system can in principle represent is not what it reliably
does. Merely being able to \emph{emit} a Turing-complete language is trivial and says
nothing about whether the right program will be found, and even the stronger formal
results are heavily hedged---a transformer's forward pass is Turing-complete only
under idealized unbounded precision and unbounded intermediate computation
\citep{perez2021}, and chain-of-thought extends its reach only within comparable
limits \citep{merrill2024cot}. Where the gap between what a model \emph{could}
represent and what it \emph{does} realize has been measured directly, it proves real
and it widens with difficulty: networks with ample capacity to fit their training data
still fail to generalize across the Chomsky hierarchy \citep{deletang2023chomsky}, and
frontier models that could in principle decompose a compositional problem instead
retreat to shortcut pattern-matching and degrade as the problem grows
\citep{dziri2023}. Expressive competence, then, is an upper bound on what might be
represented, not evidence that it will be; we treat it as a hypothesis to measure
rather than a premise to assume.

\theme{complexity hierarchy}Measuring it takes a principled notion of what makes one
processing task harder than another, and formal language theory provides one. The
Chomsky hierarchy \citep{chomsky1956} ranks a language by the machinery its recognizer
needs. A \emph{regular} language can be read with finite state alone---a regular
expression; a \emph{context-free} language needs a stack, to follow nesting and
recursion; a \emph{context-sensitive} language must enforce agreement or counting
across the whole input, as in ``declare $N$ items, then produce exactly $N$,'' which no
stack alone can do; and at the top, \emph{recursively enumerable} languages allow
arbitrary and possibly non-terminating computation. Because the jump from context-free
to context-sensitive is so wide, a finer band was singled out between them---Joshi's
\emph{mildly context-sensitive} languages \citep{joshi1985} and the LCFRS fan-out
ladder \citep{seki1991}---which captures bounded counting and cross-serial
dependencies while still parsing in polynomial time, and which is where most real data
turns out to sit. Grammatical class, however, is only half of what makes a format hard
in practice. The other half is whether a grammar is actually \emph{available}, in the
sense that someone has already designed and tested a parser for it, and the two come
apart cleanly: a context-free XML file is grammatically more complex than a regular,
ad-hoc log, yet far easier to extract, precisely because its grammar has been supplied.
Keeping intrinsic class and grammar-availability separate is what lets us attribute a
success or failure to the right cause---and, as we will see, it is the second factor,
not the first, that a harness can move.

\theme{parsing as probe}Parsing is an unusually clean place to look for this gap. It
is the first step of nearly every information-processing chain and the least examined,
yet on real inputs it already spans the whole hierarchy, from trivially regular tables
to cross-referential multi-file formats. The nearest prior measurements grade language
models on formal-language recognition across the Chomsky hierarchy as input length
grows \citep{chomskybench, relic}; we take them as a starting point but change what is
at stake. They use synthetic strings and ask the model to \emph{recognize} membership;
we use real, heterogeneous scientific output and ask the agent to \emph{build a working
artifact}---a parser or extraction pipeline that returns correct structured data. And
instead of a single accuracy number, we vary the support the agent is given and index
its failures by grammatical class, so that we can see not only where performance breaks
down but why, and what brings it back.

\theme{test case}The output we parse is real computational-chemistry data: the logs
and restart files that ab initio simulations produce. They are a demanding instance,
heterogeneous across codes, weakly standardized, and dense with exactly the counting
and cross-reference structure that occupies the fragile middle of the hierarchy---and
they raise the stakes, because a silent parsing failure here is a silent
\emph{scientific} failure, with the downstream analysis grounding on nothing at all.

\section{The system under test: an MCP parser harness}

The instrument for this study is a dual-mode scientific parsing framework built on the
Model Context Protocol (MCP), introduced recently at a community hackathon on
language-model applications in materials science and chemistry \citep{hackathon2025}.
It wraps a set of existing, independently maintained community parsers and exposes each
of them two ways at once: as an MCP tool that an agent can discover and call
interactively, and as a direct Python import that a production pipeline can depend on.
Around the parsers sits a documentation layer---per-parser descriptions of the formats
each one handles, machine-readable capability metadata, and prompt scaffolding---whose
motivating purpose was to automate the testing and deployment of parsers as they are
onboarded, lowering the cost of contributing a new parser to a shared materials
database and, more broadly, letting a community exchange parsing capability as tested,
discoverable components rather than re-implement it each time. That same layered design
is what makes the framework a clean measurement instrument. Because the parsers, their
documentation, and their tool exposure are separable, we can give the agent any subset
of them while holding everything else fixed---varying exactly how much the harness
reveals, and reading off the effect on what the agent manages to produce.

\section{Method}

\theme{two axes}The experiment varies difficulty along two axes at once. The first is
the \emph{source}: the grammatical class of the format being read---regular,
context-free, mildly context-sensitive, or context-sensitive---held apart from
grammar-availability, so that having or lacking a tested parser is measured as its own
factor rather than folded into the class. The second is the \emph{target}: how hard the artifact
the agent must \emph{produce} is. This is a generation problem, not a recognition one,
so it is deliberately \emph{not} the Chomsky ladder a second time---the output's
surface syntax (a JSON object, a Python script) is cheap to emit and, if re-parsed
downstream, cheap to recognize. What scales here is \emph{semantic} correctness: a
bounded target (fill a fixed schema) has a finite, field-checkable output space,
whereas a Turing-complete target (generate an executable artifact such as a PySCF input
script) has an unbounded one whose correctness is undecidable in general. Source
difficulty is thus a question of \emph{recognition} (the Chomsky hierarchy), target
difficulty a question of \emph{synthesis} and verifiability---two different axes, kept
separate on purpose.

\theme{arms}What we vary is how much of that harness the agent receives. In every arm
it is given the source file, the target schema, and the ability to run code; the arms
differ only in the support layered on top. At one extreme (\textsc{none}) the agent
gets no harness at all and must write its parser from scratch. From there we add
support one layer at a time: exposing the parsers as callable tools but nothing more
(\textsc{bare}), then adding their per-parser documentation (\textsc{docs}), then a
cross-parser capability map of which tool recovers which property (\textsc{full}), and
finally an annotation of the source's grammatical class together with each tool's
parsing-power ceiling (\textsc{complexity}). The step from \textsc{none} to the rest
isolates the value of the harness itself; the steps among the remaining arms isolate
the value of the documentation built on top of it.

\theme{instrumentation}Per run we record tool calls, turns, tokens, the agent's
\emph{chain-of-thought}, and a validated failure-mode taxonomy (wrong-tool,
silent-empty-accepted, parser-from-scratch, hallucinated-field, unit-error,
retry-loop, gave-up), indexed by grammatical class $\times$ target complexity $\times$
arm. Correctness is scored field-level against a reference built from the union of
succeeding parsers.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Preliminary: the grounding layer and the grammar-availability effect}

As a component measurement motivating the axes, we benchmarked four community parsers
on 1{,}104 real files across 16 simulation codes, scoring whether an atomic structure
was recovered. Reliability is low even on the primary-output file (Table~\ref{tab:rel}),
and failures are often \emph{silent} (an empty-but-valid object, no exception).
Re-cutting by grammatical class (Table~\ref{tab:class}) shows recovery falling as
class rises---but the effect is dominated by \emph{grammar-availability}: the one
structured (context-free) format with a supplied XML grammar is recovered every time,
while ``simpler'' regular logs with no canonical grammar are not. Grammatical class is
thus necessary but not sufficient; a supplied grammar is what makes extraction cheap.
The \emph{census} of formats is itself informative and skewed toward the fragile
middle: of the primary outputs, the large majority (44 of 51) are mildly
context-sensitive free-form logs, only a handful (4) are context-free formats with a
supplied grammar, and the remainder (3) are context-sensitive multi-file outputs.
The band where realized extraction is most fragile---and where the harness's
mitigation concentrates---is therefore the \emph{common} case for real ab initio
output, not an edge case.

\section{Discussion and outlook}

The harness's value is not expressive: the agent could synthesize the missing parser
itself (writing code is Turing-complete). Its value is to lower the \emph{cost} of
realizing that competence---supplying tested grammars, flagging emptiness, and telling
the agent when to escalate---so the offloadable (interface) failures shrink while the
irreducible (reasoning) failures remain. \pending{quantified from the sweep.} The
natural follow-up extends the target axis toward Turing-complete processing chains
(most real pipelines are Turing-complete end to end); parsing is the tractable first
step.

\section*{Limitations}

Preliminary results are a single database snapshot (52 entries, 16 codes); success is
field presence, not correctness against ground truth; parsers run with defaults; the
grammatical-class assignment is a documented ordinal partly confounded with program at
small $N$; and the main agent-sweep results are \pending{in progress}.
